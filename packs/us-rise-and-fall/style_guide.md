# Script style guide

This guide describes how a script for this channel is built. It is drawn from
analysis of a successful long-form history documentary format (about 1,800 to
2,200 words, around 13 minutes) and adapted for American stories.

## Shape of a script

1. **Cold open (120 to 180 words).** Start inside the story with a hard fact:
   a record, a number, a moment. Within three sentences, show what made it
   remarkable. Then plant the fall: a line that tells the viewer this did not
   last. Close the opening with a plain promise of what the video covers, for
   example: "So this is the story of X: how it began, how it was built, how it
   ran, and what finally brought it down."
2. **Setting (one chapter).** The place, the era, and the problem or
   opportunity that made the story possible. Introduce the first key person
   here.
3. **The plan and the people.** Who decided to do it, why, with what money.
4. **The build or the rise.** Construction, growth, expansion. Hard numbers.
   One primary-source quote.
5. **The peak.** What it was like at its best, ideally through a first-hand
   account from the time.
6. **The cracks.** Money trouble, accidents, competition, policy, war, changing
   habits. The hidden problem behind the public success.
7. **The fall.** What finally ended it, told plainly.
8. **What remains.** What stands today, or what replaced it.
9. **Close (80 to 140 words).** One final surprising fact, a short judgement on
   why it rose and why it fell, then a question for viewers to answer in the
   comments.

Chapters 3 to 8 can merge or split to fit the story. Aim for 5 to 8 chapters.

## Rules for every chapter

- **End on a turn.** The last line of every chapter opens a question the next
  chapter answers. Examples of the kind of line wanted: "But the cost of
  getting there would be far more than financial." / "For a while, it worked.
  Then the bills arrived."
- **One idea per paragraph.** Short paragraphs. Sentences mostly 8 to 20 words,
  with an occasional short one for weight.
- **Numbers with handles.** Give the original figure, then a comparison or a
  modern equivalent: "$4 million, about $140 million today", "taller than the
  Washington Monument".
- **Primary sources, quoted exactly.** Two to four quotes per script from
  newspapers, reports, letters or speeches of the time. Introduce each one
  with who said it and when, read it word for word, then return to narration.
  Trim quotes with "..." only. Never invent or improve a quote.
- **Name the people.** Real names, roles and one human detail each.
- **Spoken English.** Write for the ear. No parentheses, no footnotes, no
  bullet lists, no "as you can see". Spell numbers the way they are said when
  that helps the narrator ("eighteen ninety-eight" can stay as 1898).

## Things this channel never does

- No invented facts, numbers, dates or quotes. If the dossier does not support
  it, it does not go in the script.
- No "Imagine..." openings, no "Let's dive in", no "Buckle up", no "But here's
  the thing", no "In today's fast-paced world".
- No stacks of three short echoing sentences ("Same town. Same people.
  Different ending.").
- No rhetorical questions as section transitions ("So what happened next?").
  Use a statement that turns the story instead.
- No moralising about the present day. One judgement at the close is enough.
- No asking for likes or subscriptions in the body. One light line at the very
  end is allowed.

## Delivery notes for narration

- Mark a pause with a line break between paragraphs; the narrator breathes
  there.
- Put quotes on their own paragraph, starting with the speaker and date line.
